On 1 January 2026, the effective VAT rate on domestic supplies in Ghana fell from about 21.9% to 20% ([Crowe](https://www.crowe.com/gh/news/ghana-vat-reform-2026)). A shop that left a shelf price at GH¢120 now keeps GH¢100 of it instead of GH¢98.44.

That is a 1.6% improvement in net revenue, achieved by changing nothing. The shop next door, which cut its shelf price by 1.6% because it misread the reform as a mandated price cut, handed the entire gain straight back.

Tax-inclusive pricing is where small retail arithmetic goes wrong most often, and the error compounds quietly across every report you run.

## Divide, do not subtract

The tax inside a tax-inclusive price is the gross price multiplied by the rate, divided by 100 plus the rate. It is never the gross price multiplied by the rate.

At Kenya's standard rate of 16%, a shelf price of KES 1,160 contains **KES 160 of VAT**, leaving KES 1,000. Subtract 16% from KES 1,160 instead and you get KES 185.60, overstating the tax by KES 25.60 on every single sale.

On a shop turning over KES 1.5 million a month, that one keystroke pattern misstates about KES 33,000 of monthly gross profit. It also sends the wrong figure to your return, which is the more expensive half of the mistake.

## What a 1,000 shelf price actually contains

The divisor is the only number you need per market. Here is what a round tax-inclusive price of 1,000 in local currency breaks down into, at the standard rates in force in 2026.

| Market | Standard rate 2026 | Divisor | Tax in a 1,000 sale | Revenue you keep |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ghana (15% + NHIL 2.5% + GETFund 2.5%) | 20% effective | 1.20 | 166.67 | 833.33 |
| United Kingdom | 20% | 1.20 | 166.67 | 833.33 |
| Kenya | 16% | 1.16 | 137.93 | 862.07 |
| South Africa | 15% | 1.15 | 130.43 | 869.57 |
| Nigeria | 7.5% | 1.075 | 69.77 | 930.23 |

Rates confirmed for 2026 by [PwC's VAT tables](https://taxsummaries.pwc.com/quick-charts/value-added-tax-vat-rates), with Nigeria held at 7.5% under the Tax Act 2025 and South Africa at 15% after the proposed increase was withdrawn.

Print the divisor for your market and tape it to the counter. Every other calculation on this page comes from it.

## Where the law puts the tax inside the displayed price

Consumer retail prices are tax-inclusive prices. That is the rule, not a convention.

The UK is the clearest on paper: the Price Marking Order 2004 defines the selling price as the final price for a unit of a product, **including VAT and all other taxes** ([legislation.gov.uk](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2004/102)). The unit price, per kilogram or per litre, must be tax-inclusive too, though shops with a retail area under 280 m² are exempt from showing one.

In Kenya, the same logic runs through the VAT Act: where a supplier has not stated VAT separately on an invoice, the supply is deemed VAT-inclusive, and regulated price publications such as EPRA's fuel caps are quoted inclusive by law. Ghana, Nigeria and South Africa all operate on tax-inclusive consumer prices.

The practical consequence for a shopkeeper: you set the gross price, and the till works backwards from it. Setting a net price and adding tax at the end gives you shelf prices nobody can pay in cash.

## Ghana, 1 January 2026: what actually changed

Ghana's reform is the one worth understanding in detail, because it changed the arithmetic rather than the headline rate.

The VAT Act 2025 (Act 1151) and the COVID-19 Health Recovery Levy (Repeal) Act 2025 took effect on 1 January 2026 ([KPMG](https://kpmg.com/us/en/taxnewsflash/news/2026/02/ghana-vat-measures-2026-budget-enacted.html)). Four changes matter at counter level.

The 1% COVID-19 levy is gone. Parliament expects **GH¢3.7 billion returned to businesses and individuals in 2026**.

The cascading calculation is gone. Under the old method, NHIL, GETFund and the COVID levy were added to the taxable value before the 15% VAT was applied, which is why the real burden reached 21.9% rather than 21%. Now the levies sit alongside VAT on the same base, giving 20%.

NHIL and GETFund became deductible as input VAT on qualifying domestic supplies. Importers get less relief: [EY notes](https://globaltaxnews.ey.com/news/2026-0163-ghanas-parliament-enacts-several-indirect-tax-related-laws-effective-1-january-2026) that the cascading effect still applies on imports.

The VAT Flat Rate Scheme was abolished, and the registration threshold for traders in goods rose from GH¢200,000 to GH¢750,000. A retailer who was on the flat rate and is still above the new threshold now has to compute output and input VAT properly, which is a till configuration job, not an accounting one.

## The trap that costs the most: mixed VAT status

Two items, the same supplier ticket, the same shelf price, and gross profit that differs by 60%.

Take a Kenyan shop at 16%, with two products that both cost KES 1,000 net and both sell at KES 1,300 on the shelf.

| Scenario | Supplier invoice | Output VAT on sale | Gross profit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Zero-rated item, registered shop | 1,000 (no VAT) | 0 | 300.00 |
| Standard-rated item, registered shop | 1,160 (160 recoverable) | 179.31 | 120.69 |
| Standard-rated item, unregistered shop | 1,160 (not recoverable) | 0 | 140.00 |

The second line is the one that surprises people. A registered shop selling a standard-rated item at KES 1,300 keeps KES 120.69, because KES 179.31 of that price was never its money.

Note the third line too. On this single item the unregistered shop keeps more, because it charges no output VAT and its customers pay the same. That advantage disappears as soon as input VAT on a wider basket becomes recoverable, which is exactly why the registration decision has to be made per product mix rather than per instinct. ## The day you register

If you register and keep every shelf price where it is, your net revenue falls by the tax fraction. At 16%, a KES 1,000 sale becomes KES 862.07 of revenue: a **13.8% cut**.

Restoring it takes a 16% increase, to KES 1,160, not a 13.8% increase. Raising prices by the drop you measured leaves you short, and that off-by-one logic is behind a lot of first-quarter losses after registration.

Before repricing the whole shop, net off the input VAT you can now recover on purchases. On a shop buying mostly standard-rated stock, the recovery covers much of the gap and a smaller increase holds the line. On a shop buying mostly zero-rated food, it covers almost nothing. The method is the same one we use for cost increases in [repricing the shelf](/en/blog/retail-repricing-margin-guide/).

## Setting the till up so it stays right

Three settings decide whether your numbers hold.

**Store the gross price, compute the tax.** Prices entered tax-inclusive, tax derived per line at the item's own rate. A till that stores net prices and adds tax at checkout will round differently from your shelf labels.

**Tag every product with its VAT status.** Standard, zero-rated or exempt, per item, not per category. The mixed-status table above only works if the till knows which line is which.

**Round at line level, not at basket level.** Rounding the basket total hides a difference that your return will not forgive at the end of the quarter.

A till that handles this correctly also gives you the margin report that matters: profit after output VAT, by product, not turnover.
