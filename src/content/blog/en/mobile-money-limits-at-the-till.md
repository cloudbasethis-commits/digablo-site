A customer picks out a fridge at KES 260,000, taps the till number, confirms, and the payment fails. Nothing is wrong with the money. Safaricom's published ceiling is **KES 250,000 per transaction**, so the sale is ten thousand shillings too big to pass in one go ([Safaricom tariff page](https://www.safaricom.co.ke/personal/m-pesa/mpesa-charges)).

Mobile money moved about $2.09 trillion worldwide in 2025, up 23% in a year, and Africa accounted for roughly two thirds of that value, according to the GSMA's [2026 State of the Industry report](https://www.gsma.com/solutions-and-impact/connectivity-for-good/mobile-for-development/state-of-the-industry-report-on-mobile-money/). Every one of those payments passed through a cap somewhere. Most shopkeepers learn where the caps sit by losing a sale.

## There are three caps, not one

A mobile money payment has to clear three separate ceilings, and they are set independently of each other.

**Per transaction.** The largest single amount that can move at once. This is the one that blocks big-ticket sales.

**Per day.** The total a customer may move in a calendar day across everything: transfers to family, bills, airtime, and your till. A customer who has already paid school fees at lunchtime can be blocked at your counter at six, with money still in the wallet.

**Maximum balance.** The most the wallet may hold at any moment. This one matters more to you than to the customer, and we come back to it below.

Ghana adds a fourth for the lowest identity tier: a monthly ceiling on top of the daily one.

## The numbers, market by market

Caps are set by the regulator and applied by the operator, so they move with directives rather than with price lists.

| Market | Per transaction | Per day | Maximum balance | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Kenya (M-PESA) | KES 250,000 | KES 500,000 | KES 500,000 | Safaricom tariff page |
| Ghana, Minimum KYC | Within daily cap | GHS 3,000 (plus about GHS 10,000 monthly) | Not published in the same notice | Telecel Ghana support |
| Ghana, Medium KYC | Within daily cap | GHS 15,000 | Not published, no monthly cap | Telecel Ghana support |
| Ghana, Enhanced KYC | Within daily cap | GHS 25,000 | GHS 75,000 reported | Telecel Ghana support |
| Nigeria, Tier 1 wallet | Within daily cap | NGN 30,000 to 50,000, sources differ | NGN 300,000 | Youverify, MyPryme |
| Nigeria, Tier 2 wallet | Within daily cap | NGN 200,000 to 500,000, sources differ | NGN 500,000 | Youverify, GetFraudSpect |
| Nigeria, Tier 3 wallet | Provider dependent | NGN 5,000,000 or uncapped | Uncapped | Youverify, MyPryme |

Two warnings about this table. The Kenyan and Ghanaian rows come from the operators' own published pages. The Nigerian rows come from compliance guides that disagree with each other on the daily figures, and individual providers apply tighter internal limits inside a tier, so treat Nigeria as a range to verify in the customer's app rather than a fixed number.

Ghana's current figures date from a Bank of Ghana directive that lifted the daily limits from GHS 2,000, GHS 10,000 and GHS 15,000 to GHS 3,000, GHS 15,000 and GHS 25,000. Safaricom restated the Kenyan limits in October 2026 and noted they are set under Central Bank of Kenya regulation, not operator pricing.

## The cap is the customer's, not yours

This is the part that gets argued at counters. When a till payment fails on a limit, the ceiling being hit belongs to the person paying, and you cannot raise it.

You also cannot see it. The error message tells the customer more than it tells you, which is why the useful first move is to ask what the message says rather than to retry the payment. A retry on a daily cap fails identically every time and makes the queue behind think your till is broken.

What you can do is know the three caps for your market well enough to diagnose the failure in one question, then offer the right alternative immediately. A shop that does this keeps the sale. A shop that does not sends the customer to find an agent and loses them to the shop next door.

## What to do when a payment refuses

Five moves, in the order worth trying.

1. **Split within the daily cap.** If the purchase is above the per-transaction ceiling but below the daily one, two payments clear it. In Kenya, KES 260,000 goes through as 150,000 plus 110,000. Record both references against the same sale.
2. **Take part in cash.** The fastest resolution for an amount just over a cap, and the one that costs the customer nothing in extra fees.
3. **Ask for a bank transfer.** Bank rails run on their own limits, which are usually higher for a verified account. Give out the account number you actually reconcile, never a staff member's personal account.
4. **Send them to an agent, for a deposit and not a withdrawal.** Cash paid in at an agent to your own number arrives as a normal payment. A withdrawal to pay you in notes adds a cash-out fee for nothing.
5. **Hold the goods against a deposit.** For a large purchase blocked by a daily cap, a part payment today and the balance tomorrow is better than a lost sale, provided the goods stay in your store until the second payment lands.

What not to do is release the goods on a screenshot. A refused payment and a fabricated confirmation produce a similar looking screen, and the forgery is the most common counter scam in these markets, as we set out in our guide to [fake payment confirmations](/en/blog/fake-payment-confirmation-fraud/). The rule holds whatever the reason for the failure: goods move when your own till or your own registered line shows the money, with a reference you can read back.

## The tier conversation is worth having once

For a regular customer who hits a cap every month, the fix is an upgrade, and it is quick.

In Ghana the tiers are defined by what the operator holds on file. Minimum KYC needs only the Ghana Card. Medium adds next of kin and source of funds. Enhanced adds proof of address. Each step raises the daily ceiling.

In Nigeria, Tier 2 and Tier 3 wallets require both BVN and NIN linked to the account, a requirement in force since December 2023. A Tier 1 customer at your counter is capped low because of what the provider does not know about them, not because of their balance.

You are not their compliance officer. But telling a wholesale buyer once that their daily limit tracks their identity tier turns a recurring blocked sale into a solved problem.

## The ceiling that is actually yours

The balance cap applies to your till account too. In Kenya the maximum M-PESA account balance is KES 500,000, and a shop that takes most of its sales digitally and never moves the money can reach it on a strong trading day. From there, incoming payments start to fail, and the fault looks like the customer's.

The habit that prevents it is a daily or twice daily sweep to a bank account. Running the sweep as one transfer rather than as an agent withdrawal followed by a branch deposit also avoids paying two fees to move the same money, a point we worked through in [mobile money merchant fees in 2026](/en/blog/mobile-money-merchant-fees-2026/).

## What your till should record when a payment splits

A split payment is where reconciliation quietly breaks. One sale, two or three references, sometimes a cash component, and a staff member under pressure to clear a queue.

Three things make it survivable:

- **Multiple payment lines on one sale**, so a KES 260,000 fridge shows two mobile money references and not two separate sales.
- **A reference field per line**, because a transaction code is the only thing that ties your record to the operator's statement at the end of the month.
- **Offline capture**, because the till has to record the sale even when the network that confirmed the payment drops a minute later.

[digabloPos](https://pos.digablo.fr/) holds several payment lines with their references on a single sale, offline, and reports by payment method without a paid upgrade. If you are comparing options, our [POS comparison](/en/pos-comparison) flags which systems allow a split tender at all, and which force a workaround that leaves your daily total correct but your statement unreconcilable.

Caps are not going away. They are a regulator's tool, they get raised every few years, and in the meantime they are part of the counter. The shops that lose least to them are the ones where the person at the till can tell in one question which of the three ceilings just moved in front of a sale.
