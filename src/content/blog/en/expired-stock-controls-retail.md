In May 2026, NAFDAC burned 1.85 billion naira worth of expired goods in Abuja ([FTX](https://ftxcdn.xyz/2026/05/20/nafdac-destroys-expired-products-worth-over-n1-85b-in-abuja-by-olufemi-damilola-/)). That was one exercise, in one city. Every unit in that pile was bought by someone, paid for in advance, and sat on a shelf until it was worth nothing.

The shops that lost that money almost certainly ran stock takes. Counting would not have saved them.

## Why your stock take misses it

A stock take answers one question: is what is on the shelf what your records say is there. Expired goods are on the shelf. They count. The sheet balances, the variance looks healthy, and the loss sits there in plain sight wearing a barcode.

This is the structural difference between theft and expiry. Theft creates a gap between the record and the shelf, which is what [counting is designed to find](/en/blog/stock-take-small-shop-guide/). Expiry creates no gap at all. It converts stock into rubbish without moving a single unit.

Which means expiry needs its own control, running on dates rather than quantities.

## What it actually costs

Expired and spoiled stock sits in the same bucket as shrink in most shops' accounts, and it is usually the bigger line. A study of retail outlets cited in South African SMME research puts shrinkage at roughly **2.6 per cent of sales** (Chanda and Mwanza, 2023), and perishable-heavy shops run above that.

Two wider numbers set the scale. The UNEP Food Waste Index Report 2024 attributes 131 million tonnes of food waste in 2022 to retail, about 12 per cent of the global total ([UNEP](https://www.unep.org/resources/publication/food-waste-index-report-2024)). And the FAO's Food Loss Index puts sub-Saharan Africa at 21.4 per cent, the highest loss rate of any region.

Then there is the part nobody budgets for: the dates themselves are misread. The European Commission estimates up to 10 per cent of EU food waste is linked to date labelling, and in one survey of 602 adults, only 32.2 per cent correctly understood what "best before" means, while 60.6 per cent wrongly believed the product could not legally be sold after that date. Your staff are in that survey. So are your customers.

## The markdown maths

The cheapest moment to act on a near-expiry item is the moment you notice it, and the number proves it. Take an item bought at 100 and priced at 130, a 23 per cent margin typical of fast-moving groceries.

| Action, ten days before expiry | Shelf price | Cash back per unit | Against the 100 you paid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sell at full price | 130 | 130 | +30 |
| Mark down 20 per cent | 104 | 104 | +4 |
| Mark down 30 per cent | 91 | 91 | 9 lost |
| Mark down 50 per cent | 65 | 65 | 35 lost |
| Let it expire and write it off | 0 | 0 | 100 lost, plus disposal |

The break-even against cost sits at a 23 per cent discount. Below that you are still ahead; above it you are limiting damage. **Every line in that table beats the last one**, which is the whole argument for acting early rather than hoping.

Two rules follow. Mark down in one clear step rather than three timid ones, because a 10 per cent sticker on milk with four days left moves nothing. And put the markdown through the till as a discount, not as a changed price, so your margin reports still show what the product normally earns.

## Use by, best before, and the legal line

These are not the same date, and treating them as one is how shops either throw away sellable stock or sell what they must not.

A use-by date is a safety date. After it, the food is not considered safe, and selling it exposes you to the full weight of the regulator. A best-before date is a quality date: the product is expected to be at its best until then.

Nigeria's position is the most explicit in the region. Under the Counterfeit and Fake Drugs and Unwholesome Processed Foods (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act, Cap C34, selling or displaying expired products for sale carries a fine of up to 500,000 naira alongside imprisonment, and reporting on recent cases describes NAFDAC sealing premises first and asking questions afterwards. In September 2026 the agency's Director-General went further, publicly calling for life imprisonment for dealers in fake regulated products ([ThisDay](https://www.thisdaylive.com/2026/09/09/nafdac-seeks-life-imprisonment-death-penalty-for-fake-product-dealers-mops-up-7210-fake-products-across-nigeria/)).

Kenya, Ghana and South Africa each have their own regime, and the detail differs. The practical rule travels: **the shop displaying the goods carries the liability**, not the distributor who sold them to you short-dated.

## Where the dates actually slip

Four places, in order of how much money they cost.

**Receiving.** A delivery arrives with three months left when the invoice assumed nine. Nobody checks, because checking means opening cartons while a driver waits. This is the single highest-leverage control in the whole chain, and it costs thirty seconds per line.

**The back of the shelf.** New stock goes in front because it is faster. The older unit migrates backwards until it is found during a clear-out. FEFO, first expired first out, exists to stop exactly this: the unit with the nearest date is sold first, whatever day it arrived.

**Slow movers you believe in.** The imported sauce, the premium shampoo, the specialist medicine. Each one sells rarely enough that nobody tracks its date, and expensive enough that the write-off hurts twice.

**Promotions bought in bulk.** The discount on twenty cartons only pays if you sell twenty cartons before the date. Work out the weekly rate you need, then compare it to what you actually sold last month before you accept the deal.

## Terms to negotiate with suppliers

Most shopkeepers never ask, and most suppliers have an answer.

Sale or return is standard for bread, milk and yoghurt across Kenya and Nigeria: the supplier takes back unsold units on the next run. Get it in the agreement rather than the relationship, because the relationship changes when the sales rep does.

Beyond that, three asks worth making. A minimum remaining shelf life on delivery, commonly two thirds of total life. Credit on near-expiry stock in exchange for prominent display. And the right to refuse a short-dated delivery without affecting your credit terms. A supplier who refuses all three is managing their own expiry problem by moving it onto your shelf.

## What the software has to do

Three fields at receiving, and the rest follows: batch, expiry date, quantity.

With those, your system can produce the only report that matters here, a list of what expires in the next 30, 14 and 7 days, sorted by value. Without them, expiry control is a notebook someone updates when they remember.

The till side matters too. Staff need the system to flag the nearest-dated batch at the counter, and a markdown has to go through as a discount so you can measure later what the exercise cost. It has to work offline, because receiving happens at a back door where coverage is whatever it is, and a delivery logged on paper "for later" is a delivery logged never.

## Start with twenty products

Pick the twenty items that have cost you the most in write-offs over the past year. If you do not know which they are, that is already the finding.

Record expiry dates for those twenty at the next delivery. Run one list a week of what falls due inside thirty days, and mark down anything inside fourteen. Four weeks later you will have a number for what you recovered, and a short list of suppliers whose delivery dates need a conversation.

The products that keep appearing on that list are not unlucky. They are overordered, and that is a buying decision you can change on Monday.
