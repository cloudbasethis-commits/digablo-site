Type "POS" into a search bar in Lagos and you get card terminals, agent commissions and caution fees. Type the same three letters in Nairobi and you get barcode scanners, cash drawers and till systems. Same word, two completely different purchases.

OPay alone has reported over 563,000 registered POS agents in Nigeria ([NewswireNGR](https://newswirengr.com/2023/02/20/top-10-pos-agent-banking-platforms-in-nigeria/)). That is why the word drifted. For a shopkeeper, the drift is expensive: it is entirely possible to spend 50,000 naira on a "POS" and still have no idea what sold yesterday.

## The two things the word means

A POS machine moves money. POS software runs the shop. They are not substitutes, and only one of them tells you whether you are making a profit.

The machine is a payment terminal. It authorises a card or a wallet transfer, prints a slip, and settles into an account. In Nigeria it is also a cash service: customers come to the shop to withdraw, and the agent earns a commission on the float, under the agent banking model the Central Bank of Nigeria introduced in 2013.

The software is the till. It holds your product list and prices, records each sale against a payment method, decrements stock, logs which attendant served the customer, and closes the day with a figure you can check against the cash in the drawer. A card terminal does none of that. It will tell you how much card money arrived, which is a fraction of your takings and none of your margin.

Shop owners mix these up constantly, and vendors have no incentive to correct them. As one Nigerian explainer puts it, the machine is the thing you pay for while the software that powers the business is often free ([Monierate](https://monierate.com/blog/what-is-a-point-of-sale-pos-and-how-it-works-in-nigeria)).

## Why the terminal rarely comes first

Card acceptance solves a problem a small shop in Nairobi, Lagos or Accra has maybe twice a day. Stock walking out of the back door is a problem it has every week.

The money a terminal brings in is also smaller than it looks once fees are counted. Moniepoint charges 0.5 per cent on withdrawals up to 20,000 naira and a flat 100 naira above that, per Truehost's comparison. On genuine retail volumes rather than agent float, that revenue is thin.

There is a second reason to be careful, which we set out in detail in our piece on [agent banking versus merchant payments](/en/blog/agent-banking-vs-merchant-payments/): running a cash agency inside a retail shop mixes two tills, two float positions and two sets of risk. Plenty of shops do it well. None of them do it well by accident.

## Where the money goes after you buy

The purchase price is the part everyone compares. The running cost is the part that decides whether the setup survives a year.

**Paper.** Thermal rolls are listed at KSh 240 to 250 each by retailers such as [TDK Kenya](https://www.tdk.co.ke/product/thermal-paper-roll-80mm-x-80mm-price/), but a 50-roll box sells for KSh 6,000 to 7,500 on Jiji and Jumia, which works out around KSh 120 to 150 per roll. Buying rolls one at a time costs roughly double. A busy counter gets through several a week.

**Power and connectivity.** A counter built from a desktop, a powered printer and a router needs ten to fifteen times the backup capacity of a phone running the same till, which we priced out in our guide to [backup power for your till](/en/blog/till-backup-power-outages/).

**Replacement.** An all-in-one terminal is one device: when the printer head fails, the till fails with it. Separate parts cost slightly more and fail separately. For a single-counter shop, that matters more than the 5,000 shilling difference in purchase price.

## The compliance question that changes the hardware answer

Tax rules now push in the opposite direction from hardware sellers, and that is good news for your budget.

Kenya's eTIMS is largely software-based. Compliance can be met through eTIMS Lite, the eTIMS portal, the eTIMS client or an integrated POS, rather than a branded tax register, and buying a specific box is generally not a requirement ([Plannettech](https://plannettech.co.ke/etr-machine-vs-etims-in-kenya/)). Small and micro enterprises with annual turnover below KES 5 million are routed to eTIMS Lite, available on eCitizen or by dialling \*222#.

So the ETR row in the table above is often a 30,000 to 45,000 shilling purchase that a correctly chosen till makes unnecessary. Ask the vendor which invoicing route their software uses before you buy anything physical. Our article on [e-invoicing in Africa](/en/blog/e-invoicing-africa-retail-2026/) covers what the rules require market by market.

## A buying order that works

1. **Install till software on a phone you already own and run it for two weeks.** Real transactions, real staff, real closing figures. The cost is zero and the lesson is specific to your shop.
2. **Buy a printer only when you know your receipt volume.** Two weeks of data tells you whether 58mm Bluetooth is enough or you need an 80mm counter unit.
3. **Add the scanner when your product list passes a few hundred lines.** Below that, typing is faster than scanning badly labelled stock.
4. **Add card acceptance last, and only when customers ask twice.** Wallet and cash cover most counters; the terminal is a response to demand, not a way to create it.
