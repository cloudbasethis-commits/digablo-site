In a TechnoServe study of Nairobi dukas, roughly 8 percent of inventory expired on the shelf before it could be sold. Not stolen. Not miscounted at the till. Bought with the owner's own working capital, then thrown away.

That figure is the argument for counting stock, and it is also the reason most owners dread it. A stock take is usually sold as a theft hunt. It is actually a measurement, and theft is rarely the biggest line in the result.

## A stock take answers one question

It tells you the difference between what your records say you should have and what is physically on the shelf. Everything useful comes from that single number.

Without a count, you have purchase records and sales records, and you assume the gap between them is stock. That assumption drifts. Six months of small receiving errors, a broken crate nobody logged, three customers who took goods on credit without it being written down, and the figure on your screen stops describing your shop.

The variance is not an accusation. It is a diagnosis, and different causes leave different fingerprints.

## Theft is rarely the biggest line

External theft accounts for 25 percent of total retail loss, according to the [2026 Beyond Shrink study](https://www.sensormatic.com/resources/pr/2026/beyond-shrink-report-press-release) from IHL Group and Sensormatic Solutions, which surveyed 1,421 retailers across 12 countries. The remaining 75 percent comes from elsewhere in the business.

The number moves with size, and it moves in the direction that matters here. External theft is 26 percent of loss for retailers above one billion dollars in revenue, but **only 17 percent for retailers under 250 million dollars**. The smaller the business, the less of its loss is someone walking out with goods.

One comparison from the same study is worth keeping in mind at the counter: administrative process errors cost an average of 103.40 dollars per incident, against 107.00 dollars for employee theft. Nearly identical damage, and the paperwork version happens far more often.

## Where small-shop stock actually goes

| Cause | What it looks like in a duka or provision store | What the count shows | What fixes it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Expiry | Slow lines at the back, dusty packaging | Physical count matches, but goods are unsellable | Count with expiry dates; order smaller, more often |
| Receiving errors | Supplier short-delivers, nobody checks | Shortage concentrated on one supplier's lines | Count goods in against the invoice, at delivery |
| Unrecorded credit | Regulars take goods, pay later, nothing written | Shortage on staples with cash intact | A credit ledger per customer, enforced |
| Breakage and spillage | Bottles, eggs, sachets | Small, steady shortage on fragile lines | A waste log, even on paper |
| Unit conversion | Carton bought, pieces sold | Variance that swings positive then negative | One stock unit per product, conversion at receiving |
| Till manipulation | Sale rung at the wrong price, difference pocketed | Stock short, cash correct | Price locked in software, variance reports |
| Shoplifting | Small, high-value items near the door | Shortage on specific lines, no pattern in cash | Layout, visibility, counting those lines weekly |

Read the table in that order, not in reverse. Owners who start with the last two rows tend to accuse staff, find nothing, and lose a cashier who was not the problem.

## How often to count

Count the fast movers weekly and the long tail quarterly. A full annual count is the standard answer and the one most shops never actually finish.

BFA Global's observational work in Nairobi found that about [15 percent of products generate roughly 80 percent of revenue](https://bfaglobal.com/fibr/insights/a-day-in-the-life-of-a-shopkeeper/) in a small shop. Those lines are where your capital sits, where it turns over, and where a leak compounds fastest. Twenty to forty products, counted every Monday morning, takes twenty minutes once the habit is set.

For everything else, four counts a year is enough. Spread them: one category per week, rotating, so no single session ever takes a full day.

## The procedure that works behind a counter

Six steps, in this order.

1. **Pick a boundary.** One category, one shelf, one supplier's range. Not the whole shop.
2. **Freeze that category.** For the twenty minutes of counting, nothing moves in or out. If a customer buys a counted item, write it on a separate slip and apply it afterwards.
3. **Count blind.** The counter does not see the expected quantity. This is the step most shops skip, and skipping it quietly destroys the value of the whole exercise, because people confirm a printed number instead of counting.
4. **Use two people on high-value lines.** One counts, one records. Not the person who normally handles that stock.
5. **Reconcile the same day.** A count sheet that sits until the weekend is a count sheet that gets adjusted from memory.
6. **Set a variance threshold and investigate above it.** Half a percent on a staple is noise. Eight percent on one supplier's lines is a conversation with that supplier.

### Counting units when you buy cartons

Decide one stock unit per product and hold the line. If you sell cooking oil by the sachet, your stock unit is the sachet, and a carton of 24 becomes 24 units at receiving.

Mixing units is the most common source of meaningless variance in shops that break bulk. We covered the pricing side of this in [what selling a carton by the piece really costs](/en/blog/breaking-bulk-unit-pricing/); the counting side is simpler but just as unforgiving. One unit, recorded conversion, no exceptions.

## Reading the variance

A shortage with cash intact and a shortage with cash short are two different problems.

**Stock short, cash correct.** Goods left without being rung up. Credit sales nobody wrote down, staff taking items, or deliveries to customers handled informally. Start with the credit ledger, because it is the most common and the least sinister. Our guide on [the controls that keep a shop solvent](/en/blog/selling-on-credit-controls/) covers how to tighten that without losing the customers.

**Stock short, cash also short.** Usually a pricing or till problem rather than a stock problem. Items sold below the set price, voids used to hide a sale, or a price list that lives in someone's head.

**Stock correct, margin falling.** Nothing is missing. Your buying price moved and your shelf price did not.

**Stock over.** Yes, it happens, and it is not good news. Over-counts usually mean goods received twice in the system, or a return processed twice. The same error that creates a phantom unit also tells you to reorder something you already have.

## The cost of not counting is a stockout, not a theft

Being out of stock on a fast mover costs more than shrink, and the evidence is unusually strong on this.

Economists Michael Kremer, Jean Lee, Jonathan Robinson and Olga Rostapshova tracked 45 small shops across 11 towns in Western Kenya and estimated an [average annualised real return of 113 percent on added inventory](https://www.frbsf.org/wp-content/uploads/robinson.pdf), with a median shop at 36 percent. The study is about capital rather than counting, but the implication is direct: a staple missing from your shelf for a week is a far larger loss than the few units that walked out the door.

That is the real return on a weekly count. Not catching a thief. Knowing what to reorder before the shelf is empty.

## What the software has to do

Three requirements, and the third eliminates most options.

**Count on the device in your hand.** Entering a count on a phone, at the shelf, beats a paper sheet typed up later. The retyping step is where errors enter.

**Convert units at receiving.** The software holds the carton-to-piece factor, so a delivery of 10 cartons becomes 240 sachets without arithmetic at the counter.

**Work offline.** A count happens before opening or after closing, often in a back room, and network coverage there is whatever it is. Software that needs a connection to save a count sheet will lose one eventually, and a lost count is worse than no count, because it burns the habit. This matters as much for counting as it does at the till, as we argued in [offline POS software](/en/blog/offline-pos-software/).

## Start this week

Pick your twenty fastest-moving products. Count them tomorrow before opening, blind, on one sheet. Compare against your records, and write the variance down somewhere you will still have it next month.

The first number will be uncomfortable and largely uninformative. The second one, four weeks later, is where the pattern appears, and the pattern is what you act on.
