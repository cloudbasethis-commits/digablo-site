Moniepoint disbursed more than $700 million to Nigerian MSMEs in 2025, reported separately as over one trillion naira across roughly 70,000 businesses ([The Guardian Nigeria](https://guardian.ng/business-services/moniepoint-disburses-700m-loans-to-msmes-in-2025/)). That is about 14 million naira per business, lent largely to shops that could not have raised a tenth of it from a traditional bank five years ago.

Nothing about those shops changed. What changed is that their takings became readable.

## Why your till record is now the collateral

Lenders stopped asking retailers for title deeds because a payment history predicts repayment better than an asset nobody wants to repossess. The IFC puts the MSME finance gap in emerging markets at **$5.7 trillion**, climbing to $8 trillion once informal enterprises are counted, roughly 19% of GDP across the 119 economies it measured ([IFC](https://www.ifc.org/en/insights-reports/2026/msme-finance-gap-in-emerging-markets-and-developing-economies)). In South Africa, only about 5% of formalised businesses reach credit at all.

That gap exists because of evidence, not risk appetite. A shop turning over a steady amount every day is a reasonable borrower; it simply had no way to prove it. A till that logs each sale, with the same identifier, over months, is that proof.

Which also means the reverse holds. If your sales are split between a till number, a personal wallet and cash that never gets recorded, you are invisible to exactly the lenders built to serve you.

## The factor rate is where the cost hides

A merchant cash advance does not quote interest. It quotes a factor, and a factor is not comparable to an annual rate until you convert it.

Factors sit between **1.1 and 1.5** in the UK and South African markets, with YouLend quoted at 1.03 to 1.35 and iwoca describing 1.2 to 1.5 as typical ([Funding Agent](https://www.fundingagent.co.uk/post/youlend-merchant-cash-advance)). Borrow 1,000,000 at a factor of 1.3 and you repay 1,300,000, whenever that happens.

The conversion that matters: take the fee, divide it by the total repayable, then multiply by 365 divided by the number of days to repay. A factor of 1.35 collected in 180 days lands near 53%.

| Factor | Repaid over | Fee on 1,000,000 | Approximate annual cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.10 | 90 days | 100,000 | 37% |
| 1.10 | 180 days | 100,000 | 18% |
| 1.30 | 180 days | 300,000 | 47% |
| 1.35 | 180 days | 350,000 | 53% |
| 1.35 | 90 days | 350,000 | 105% |

Read the two 1.35 rows together. **The same fee costs twice as much when the money comes back twice as fast**, because the fee is fixed while the time you had the funds is not. This is the opposite of a bank loan, where paying early saves you money. Here, early repayment saves nothing at all, a point iwoca states plainly in its own guide.

So the question to ask a merchant lender is never "what is the rate". It is: what is the total I repay, and over how many days do you expect to collect it.

## The sweep is the risk nobody explains upfront

With a card or till advance, a percentage of every sale is diverted before it reaches you, often 10% to 20%. On paper this is the product's best feature, since the repayment falls automatically in a slow week. In a shop it bites differently.

Your margin does not flex with your takings. Rent, wages and the next restock do not drop 15% when sales drop 15%. If a fifth of every shilling that arrives is already spoken for, the shop runs on 80% of its cash flow while carrying 100% of its fixed costs. That works if the money bought stock that turns quickly. It fails if it covered a hole.

Three tests before signing:

**Does the money buy something that sells inside the repayment window?** Stock that turns in three weeks against a 30 day recovery is sound. A generator repaid out of daily sweeps is not, because it produces no additional sales for months.

**Can the shop survive the sweep during its slowest fortnight?** Not the average month. The worst one.

**Is it a second loan while the first is open?** Stacking advances is the single most common way shops in Lagos and Nairobi lose control of their cash, and it looks reasonable each time because each individual approval is easy.

The discipline is the same one that keeps a customer credit book safe, which we covered in [selling on credit](/en/blog/selling-on-credit-controls/). Borrowing and lending fail for the identical reason: nobody wrote down the exposure.

## How to make your data lendable before you need it

Underwriting reads consistency, not size. Four habits do most of the work, and none of them cost anything.

**One till, one identifier.** Takings that arrive across a business till, a personal mobile money number and an envelope cannot be assembled into a history. Pick the business channel and route everything through it, cash sales included.

**Record cash as carefully as mobile money.** In most shops the digital half of sales looks weak only because the cash half was never entered. A lender reading your till sees a smaller business than you run.

**Start six months before you need the facility.** Both Kenyan products require more than six months of till activity, and Moniepoint wants six weeks of consistent account use. There is no way to compress that window retroactively.

**Keep the trail unbroken through outages.** A till that stops recording when the network drops leaves gaps precisely in your busiest hours, which is why offline capture matters more here than it first appears. We go into it in [offline POS software](/en/blog/offline-pos-software/).
