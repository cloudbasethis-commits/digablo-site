A shop in Lagos sells a carton of 24 at a 20 percent markup, counts the cash, and walks to the wholesaler the next morning to replace it. The wholesaler wants more than the full takings from that carton. Nothing was stolen and nothing was mispriced on the shelf. The shop simply priced on what it had paid, in a market where food prices rose 17.52 percent in the year to June 2026 according to [Trading Economics data on Nigeria](https://tradingeconomics.com/nigeria/inflation-cpi).

That gap, between the price a shop paid and the price it will pay next, is where small retailers lose money without ever seeing a loss in their records.

## Price on replacement cost, not on what you paid

Set every shelf price from what the next delivery of that item will cost, not from the invoice sitting in the drawer. Historic cost tells you what happened. Replacement cost tells you whether you can stay in business.

The arithmetic is unforgiving. A shop running on a 15 percent markup in a market with 15 percent annual cost inflation is, in real terms, working for free: each cycle it recovers its costs and nothing more. Push the markup to 25 percent and the real return is roughly 10 percent before rent, staff and power.

This is also why a shop can be profitable on paper and short of stock at the same time. Profit calculated on historic cost includes an inflation gain that was never really earned, and that money has already left as inventory the shop can no longer afford to replace at the same volume.

## Markup and margin are not the same number

Markup is a percentage of cost. Gross margin is a percentage of selling price. The same price gives two different figures, and the difference grows as prices rise.

| Markup on cost | Gross margin on selling price |
|---|---|
| 10% | 9.1% |
| 20% | 16.7% |
| 25% | 20.0% |
| 33% | 24.8% |
| 50% | 33.3% |
| 100% | 50.0% |

A shop owner who says "I work on 30 percent" usually means markup, while the supplier quoting a 30 percent margin requirement means something else entirely. **Agree which number you are talking about before you agree the price.** Pricing a line at a 30 percent markup when the business plan assumed a 30 percent margin leaves a shortfall of about 7 points of revenue on every sale.

## What inflation actually is in your market in 2026

The three biggest retail markets in anglophone Africa are not in the same place in 2026, so the same pricing rule produces very different outcomes.

| Market | Latest headline inflation | Component that hits a shop hardest |
|---|---|---|
| Nigeria | 15.39% (August 2026) | Food 17.52% in the year to June 2026 |
| Kenya | 6.5% (July 2026) | Food 9.4% and transport 16.5% (May 2026) |
| Ghana | 3.8% (January 2026) | Down from 5.4% in December 2025 |

Kenya's figures come from [KNBS consumer price releases](https://www.knbs.or.ke/reports/consumer-price-indices-and-inflation-rates-july-2026/); Ghana's decline to 3.8 percent was [reported in February 2026](https://allafrica.com/stories/202602050102.html) as the lowest level since 2021.

Two lessons sit in that table. First, a Ghanaian shop that kept a 2024 repricing habit is now overpricing against a market where costs are nearly flat, and losing volume to the shop next door. Second, the national number is never your number. A duka whose turnover is mostly maize flour, cooking oil and transport-heavy bulky goods faces Kenya's 9.4 percent food figure and its 16.5 percent transport figure, not the 6.5 percent headline.

Track your own basket. Take the twenty lines that make up most of your sales, record the unit cost on every delivery, and read the trend from that. It takes a few minutes per delivery and it beats any published index for your purposes.

## Currency moves faster than the price index

For anything imported, the exchange rate is the real cost driver, and it moves weekly rather than annually.

The naira traded in a band of roughly 1,350 to 1,430 to the dollar through 2025 and into 2026. On 1 October 2026 the official CBN rate was around 1,329 while the parallel market sat near 1,384, a gap of about 4 percent. A shop whose supplier settles imports at the parallel rate while its own prices were set from an official-rate landed cost is absorbing that gap on every unit.

The practical rule for imported lines: price from the rate at which your supplier actually buys foreign currency, and review those lines when the gap between the two rates widens by more than a couple of points.

## Ghana's January 2026 VAT change reset every shelf price

The Value Added Tax Act, 2025 took effect on 1 January 2026, and it changed the cost base of Ghanaian retail rather than just the paperwork.

The registration threshold for suppliers of goods rose to 750,000 cedis, the VAT Flat Rate Scheme was abolished, cascading levies were removed, and the effective rate settled at 20 percent, made up of 15 percent VAT plus a 2.5 percent health levy and a 2.5 percent education levy, as [Crowe Ghana sets out](https://www.crowe.com/gh/news/ghana-vat-reform-2026). There is no threshold at all for service providers, who must register from day one.

A shop that was on the flat rate scheme and has not rebuilt its prices since January is now working from numbers that no longer describe its tax position. That is a repricing job, not an accounting one.

## Movement or margin: the trade-off most shops get wrong

Return on stock money is margin multiplied by how often the stock turns, and the fast cheap line usually wins.

| Line | Markup on cost | Stock turns per year | Gross profit per 100 of stock money |
|---|---|---|---|
| Imported cosmetics | 60% | 3 | 180 |
| Sachet staples | 14% | 24 | 336 |

The sachet line carries less than a quarter of the markup and returns almost twice as much on the same working capital. [Omniretail's research on Nigerian FMCG](https://omniretail.africa/insights-report/decoding-fmcg-in-nigeria/) found retailers ranking movement first (45 percent) ahead of margins (33 percent), which looks like indifference to profitability until you do this calculation. They are right, as long as they know their turns.

Most shops do not. Turns require a stock count and a sales record for the same period, which is exactly the data a point-of-sale system produces as a by-product and a cash drawer does not.

## How often to reprice, and what to touch

Reprice the top 20 lines by volume weekly, the rest of the catalogue monthly, and any item on the spot when a delivery arrives at a unit cost more than 5 percent above the previous one.

Three habits make this survivable for a single-handed shop. Round to a price customers can pay without change, because an unroundable price costs you more in friction than it earns in margin. Move cost increases on fast lines immediately and slow lines gradually, since the fast lines are where the money actually sits. And never reprice the whole shop in one day: customers notice a general increase and forgive a specific one.
