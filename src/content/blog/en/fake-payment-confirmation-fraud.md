The customer holds up a phone. The screen shows a confirmation with your business name, the right amount, today's date and a transaction code. Two bags of cement leave the yard. The money never arrives, and by the time you notice at close of day the pickup is gone.

That message was edited on a free app. Kenyan press reports describe fraudsters using message editing tools from the Play Store to change the date, time and amount on a genuine past confirmation, a trick [The Kenya Times](https://thekenyatimes.com/crime/new-trick-scammers-are-using-for-m-pesa-message-cons/) documented against landlords and traders. The forgery costs nothing to produce and takes about forty seconds.

## Why a message on a customer's phone proves nothing

A confirmation message is evidence about a phone, not about your account. It can be edited after the fact, composed from scratch and sent from an ordinary mobile number rather than the provider short code, or produced as a screenshot that never came from the provider at all.

Your record of a payment is your own system: the merchant app, the provider statement, or the payment line in your POS. Everything else is a claim made by the person asking you to hand over goods. Shops that absorb this one sentence into counter practice remove most of this loss category without buying anything.

## The five patterns worth drilling into staff

### 1. The edited confirmation

An old genuine message, altered. The giveaway is the sender: a real M-Pesa, MoMo or Airtel Money confirmation arrives from the provider short code, not from a phone number. A 2026 guide to [M-Pesa scams](https://paybillke.com/guides/mpesa-scams-2026) lists this as the first red flag to teach counter staff.

### 2. The payment to a personal number

Staff tell the customer to send to their own wallet, or a fraudster poses as staff and gives their number. The customer pays in good faith, the sale completes, and nothing reaches the business. This is the pattern that survives longest because the customer never complains.

### 3. The reversal after the goods leave

The payment lands, the goods go out, and a reversal request follows within the hour. It works best on person to person transfers, which is exactly why a merchant till matters more than convenience.

### 4. The pending transaction

"Network is slow, it will reflect shortly." Sometimes true. Sometimes the transaction was never initiated. The answer is the same either way: goods move when the payment appears in your system, not when a customer promises it will.

### 5. The overpayment refund

A customer "accidentally" sends more than the price and asks for the balance back in cash. The original credit is later reversed or was never real, and the cash is gone. Bank of Ghana's fraud taxonomy lists fraudulent transaction reversals among its named mobile money fraud types.

## What the fraud data actually shows

Digital payment fraud is falling in Nigeria while the average loss per incident rises. [NIBSS](https://nibss-plc.com.ng/nibss-digital-payment-fraud-drops-51-to-%E2%82%A625-85b-in-2025/) reported 25.85 billion naira of losses in 2025, down 51 per cent from 52.26 billion naira in 2024, though the 2024 total was inflated by a single 31.1 billion naira incident involving one entity. Incidents numbered 67,518, Lagos accounted for 63.43 per cent of activity, and the **average loss per successful incident climbed to 44,454 naira from 40,488**.

Ghana shows where the volume sits. The Bank of Ghana's 2024 fraud report, as [reported by News Ghana](https://www.newsghana.com.gh/ghana-bank-fraud-cases-rise-to-16733-in-2024/), counted 16,733 fraud cases across the financial sector, up 5 per cent, with roughly 99 million cedis at risk. Banks recorded 716 incidents. Payment service providers, which carry mobile money, recorded 15,673, a 7 per cent annual increase. The fraud has moved to where the transactions are.

Both figures cover entire financial sectors rather than retail alone. Read them as a measure of how industrial the attempt volume has become, not as your expected annual loss.

## The check that settles each pattern

| Pattern | What the customer shows | What it actually proves | The check that settles it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Edited confirmation | A message with correct amount and name | Nothing, the text is editable | Sender is the provider short code, and the payment appears in your merchant app |
| Personal number payment | A genuine confirmation | Someone was paid, not your business | The recipient name on the message matches your registered till |
| Reversal after collection | A completed payment | Payment at that moment only | Payment into the merchant till, never a personal wallet |
| Pending transaction | A promise, or a spinner | Nothing | Your own balance or statement, before goods move |
| Overpayment refund | A credit above the price | A credit that may not survive | Hold the refund until the credit clears settlement |

Each check takes under thirty seconds. That is the entire trade off: half a minute of friction per disputed sale against a loss the size of a day's margin.

## Reversals, and what is genuinely reversible

Providers keep a reversal path for money sent to the wrong recipient. Safaricom publishes one for M-Pesa, reachable by message to 456 or a call to 234, as summarised in this [reversal guide](https://pesatrail.com/mpesa-reversal-guide.html). That path exists for honest mistakes, and fraudsters use it as a tool.

The practical consequence for a shop is narrow and useful: a payment into a registered merchant till goes through merchant settlement, while a transfer into a personal number sits in the person to person dispute flow. Accepting personal wallets at the counter does not just hide revenue from your books, it puts every sale inside the mechanism most easily reversed against you.

## What to configure in the till

Most of this is settings, not spending. Our guide to [employee theft at the till](/en/blog/till-fraud-stock-loss-controls/) covers the internal side of the same problem, and the configuration overlaps.

- **One payment line per provider.** M-Pesa, MoMo and Airtel Money each get their own line, never bundled into cash. A sale you cannot attribute to a provider is a sale you cannot reconcile.
- **A mandatory reference field.** The cashier types the transaction code into the sale. It forces them to read the message properly, and it gives you a key to match against the statement.
- **Daily reconciliation.** Match the provider statement to your POS payment lines every evening, the routine we set out in the [M-Pesa merchant guide](/en/blog/m-pesa-pos-guide/). Monthly reconciliation finds the same loss eight weeks too late.
- **Named logins.** When a cashier releases goods on an unverified payment, the exception should carry a name, not a shared PIN.
- **An offline rule.** When the network drops, your POS should queue the sale and flag the payment as unverified rather than letting staff improvise. Verification resumes on reconnection.

## The counter drill

Teach it as five lines, posted at the till:

1. Read the sender, not the message. Provider short code or no sale.
2. Read the recipient name. It must be the shop's till.
3. Check the merchant app or balance, not the customer's screen.
4. Goods move after the payment appears in our system.
5. No exceptions by phone call, including from the owner.

The fifth line matters more than it looks. Social engineering at the counter usually arrives as authority, and Bank of Ghana's head of currency management put it plainly in a [MyJoyOnline interview](https://www.myjoyonline.com/the-fraudsters-dont-use-hacking-they-play-on-our-minds-bogs-cab-beyuo-on-mobile-money-fraud/): the fraudsters do not hack, they work on the mind.
